%% Abstract section.
\abstract{
    Graphical grammars distinguish from heuristic typologies in constituting closed formal systems whose product diversity arises from recombination rather than from \emph{de novo} redesign.
    In practice, the scope of an implementation is constrained both by what options have been implemented for each component (for example, the polar coordinatization, the grouped count statistic, and the rectangle geometric element) and by what combinations are enabled (these three yielding a pie graphic).
    In the interest of flexibility, implementations are generally permissive of syntactically valid combinations.
    However, within some domains, universal or conditional rules may be in place to ensure that graphics faithfully or efficiently communicate important properties of the input data, such that grammatical implementations may compromise their integrity by permitting violations of these rules.
    I will present geometric data analysis as a motivating case study for this situation.
    Visualizations of point clouds, and especially the use of biplots, relies universally on a unitary aspect ratio and conditionally on data sphering for plot elements to approximate geometric relationships among data elements.
    Rather than a limitation of graphical grammar, these should be viewed as opportunities to explore the phrase spaces of different graphical domains.
    Indeed, the geometric constraints above enable the meaningful use of two families of statistical transformation---projections of constructions in higher dimensions and constructions in relation to referent points---that would otherwise produce uninterpretable graphics.
    This leads me to argue for a change in language from Wilkinson's assertion that, while a grammatical system can produce hideous graphics, it cannot produce meaningless ones.
    I suggest that meaningfulness and meaninglessness are not mathematical properties secured by formal rules but semantic properties vested with cultural significance, and that graphical grammars must adapt to the culture of a domain in order to serve as trustworthy tools for communicating information within it.
} % end of abstract

In his book introducing the grammar of graphics (GoG), Wilkinson \cite{Wilkinson2005} emphasizes the value not only to the rigor but also to the extensibility of taking a grammatical, in opposition to a typological, approach to statistical graphics:

\begin{quote}
The remarkable consequence of building a closed formal system is that, while it solves more complex applied problems, it can appear more adaptive to the user. Paradoxically, closed systems often behave more ``openly'' than open systems.
\end{quote}

% NOTE: He argues that this is due in large part to our limited bandwidth to exercise the full freedom of choice to exhaust the possibility space, perhaps a form of choice paralysis.
This flexibility assumes and relies on a permissiveness toward the ``phrase space'' of graphics that may be realized through syntactically valid combinations.
Indeed, Wilkinson asserts that, while his system can produce many hideous graphics, it cannot produce meaningless ones.
On my reading, the primary focus, in the interest of meaningfulness, is on the mathematical determinism connecting the data to the geometric elements of a graphic---ultimately defined as a visual representation of the abstract \emph{graph} of a mathematical function.

To be sure, Wilkinson details how meaningful graphics must also account for human perceptual tendencies and cultural norms.
In popular implementations, led by Wickham \cite{Wickham2009}, most directives toward good practice take the form of defaults (e.g.~different color palettes for different variable types) and warnings (e.g.~that opacity should not encode a discrete variable).
Wilkinson also posits certain near-universal constraints, for example that the plotting window should be sized to fit the input data, even if only summary elements are visualized.

What is missing in this account, i believe, is a reckoning with dialect:
Certain disciplines use specialized graphics whose uniquely structured content imposes specialized constraints, so that extending the grammatical approach to produce them requires constraining the combinatorial freedom it makes possible.
Rather than choosing between an uninhibited extension that enables misleading graphics and a duplicative re-implementation for each domain, we should design and adopt formal principles of fidelity between important properties of the data to the graphical design patterns that encode them.

\section{Geometric Data Analysis}

GDA is a diverse family of techniques for analyzing data represented as a \emph{point cloud}---a finite multisubset $X \subset (\R^d, L^2)$---and for transforming other data types into point clouds for analysis.
The points $x_i \in X$ may be attributed \emph{masses} $m_i$, and the centroid of $X$ is taken as the origin, so that the point \emph{inertias} $m_i \lVert \vec{x} \rVert \vec{x}_i$ sum to zero.
A foundational step in GDA is the decomposition of inertia via singular value decomposition (SVD):
Taking $X_{n \times m}$ to be centered and $r \leq min(n,m)$, we obtain
\[ X = U_{n \times r} \Sigma_{r \times r} {V_{m \times r}}^\top \]
with coordinatewise inertias $\diag(\Sigma) = (\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_r)$.
The point cloud $F = U\Sigma$ is a rotation of an optimal approximation to $X$, with equality when $r = \min(n,m)$; the column vectors $\vec{v}_1,\ldots,\vec{v}_r$ of $V$ each maximize $\Var(\proj_{v_i}F_{i-1})$, where $F_{i-1}$ is the {\bfseries normal space/orthogonal complement} to the span $\langle \vec{v}_1, \ldots, \vec{v}_{i-1} \rangle$ (starting with $F_0 = F$); and each $\Var(\proj_{v_i}F_{i-1}) = {\sigma_i}^2$.
SVD thus obtains an ordered set of \emph{artificial coordinates} for the proxy point cloud $F$.

Two of the most widely used GDA techniques are principal components analysis (PCA), which obtains $X$ by centering and scaling numeric case--variable data, and correspondence analysis (CA), which obtains a matrix of standardized residuals from bivariate count data.
These techniques are called \emph{ordination} as opposed to regression (being unsupervised rather than supervised) and \emph{gradient analysis} as opposed to cluster analysis (yielding continuous rather than discrete artificial variables).
The artificial coordinates (\emph{principal coordinates} along \emph{principal axes}) are not specified from a model but derived from data; because they carry no intrinsic meaning, they are sometimes called \emph{scaffolding}.

The SVD supports a unique statistical graphic, the \emph{biplot}, introduced by Gabriel \cite{Gabriel1971}.
Because the matrices $U$ and $V$ share their $r$ artificial column variables, row markers for both (proxy row and column markers for $X$) can be overlaid on those axes.
When $F$ and $V$ are overlaid, the scalar product of the $i^\text{th}$ row marker and the $j^\text{th}$ column marker approximates $x_{ij}$; ditto $U$ and $G = VD$.
In general, a biplot overlay of $UD^a$ and $VD^b$ is called \emph{balanced} when $a + b = 1$, so that row--column inner products approximate measurements.
The inertia-laden matrices $F$ and $G$ are said to contain \emph{principal coordinates} for the rows and columns of $X$, while $U$ and $V$ contain \emph{standard coordinates} of unit magnitude.
When a matrix factor is represented in principal coordinates, the graphic approximates its geometry in the standard coordinate space of the other factor:
In PCA, distances between the markers of $F$ approximate those between the rows of $X$ in the space of measured variables, while inner products and cosines between the markers of $G$ approximate covariances and correlations between the columns of $X$ across the case measurements.
In CA, distances between markers of $F$ and of $G$ approximate $\chi^2$ distances between row and column profiles in the count table.

Greenacre \cite{Greenacre2010} uses biplots to surface the mathematical unity of these techniques.
However, this unity is rarely reflected in software packages.
Implementations of PCA, CA, and several other techniques in \pkg{stats} and \pkg{MASS}, for example, share no source code beyond \texttt{base::svd()}, accept dissimilar arguments even for equivalent parameters (e.g.~the rank $r$), and produce output classes with few shared methods.
My attempt to harmonize these tools through \pkg{ordr}, a tidyverse-inspired API that foregrounds this common theory, is the impetus for the remaining sections.

\section{Tensions between GDA and GoG}

Point clouds are more homogeneous and rigid mathematical abstractions than the case--variable tables for which most statistical graphics are designed.
Most conspicuously, the coordinates must be equally scaled to faithfully represent geometric relationships among the markers.
This need has not, to my knowledge, received focused attention in the GoG space.
For example, while Wilkinson showcases such graphics as Voronoi polygons and sun glyphs that rely for their interpretations on a unit aspect ratio, he only briefly mentions that this choice should be informed by context.
On the implementation side, \pkg{ggplot2} provides the coordinatization ggproto \texttt{CoordFixed} with default \texttt{ratio = 1}, but its invocation is the responsibility of the user.
This mismatch between domain and convention has enabled data analysts to publish biplots that misrepresent the geometry of their data.

Some default and hardcoded settings of \pkg{ggplot2} are also at odds with best practices in GDA visualization:
The artificial coordinates used as scaffolding rarely warrant labels, let alone tick marks or grid lines; instead, Gower et al. \cite{Gower2011} recommend that variable axes be incorporated as graphical elements within the plot window.
%These axes must also be calibrated to reflect the transformations applied to the input data, which requires that the statistics governing those transformations (variable means and standard deviations) be calculated and preserved.
Like coordinate axes, these variable axes must also be calibrated according to any transformations applied to the input data (as in PCA and CA), which requires that the families and parameters of these transformations be made available to the geometric constructor.

Finally, as imbalanced biplots are strongly discouraged, rigorous graphical software should inhibit such choices of the exponents $a$ and $b$.
Biplot-specific programs usually hide or protect this choice from the user:
\texttt{stats::biplot.princomp()} passes the argument \texttt{scale} to $b$ and \texttt{1 - scale} to $a$, while
\pkg{biplotEZ} provides the character-valued argument \texttt{CA(variant)} and the logical-valued argument \texttt{PCA(correlation.biplot)} that specify row-principal, column-principal, or symmetric (neither-principal) overlays.
Because \pkg{ggplot2} and similar GoG implementations generally determine plot layers from one data set at a time, this balance is again the responsibility of the user.

\section{Contributions of GDA to GoG}

The aforementioned tensions should be taken not as intrinsic to the grammatical approach but as an opportunity to advance theory and practice.
The \pkg{ggplot2} extension community in particular maintains a tradition of embracing such challenges.
I wish to highlight three contributions i believe GDA has to make to GoG, with preliminary implementations in \pkg{ordr} and its spinoff \pkg{gggda}.

First, it is often of interest in GDA to visually qualify how well the geometry in the first artificial dimensions (those used to plot) captures that of the full space.\footnote{This is quantified as a ratio of partial to total variances, called \emph{adequacy}.}
One approach is to assemble a minimal spanning tree in high dimensions and overlay its projection \cite{Jolliffe2002}.
Such layers require that the statistical transformation object have access to the full point cloud---inconsistent if not impossible in GoG as originally conceived.
Drawing from a tradition of set-valued aesthetics tracing back to \pkg{ggmosaic}, \pkg{gggda} provides a mechanism to pass coordinate data of arbitrary dimension for statistical transformation before projecting for geometric construction.

Second, the unadjusted overlay of two marker sets can yield messy biplots.
A common adjustment for PCA biplots is to offset calibrated variable axes from the dense core of case markers.
This allows for clearer projection from select markers onto select axes (yielding inner products).
To try and err at satisfying offsets is impractical, and algorithms are available that work in most cases; but they require at least summary information about secondary data---the cases---to compute graphical elements for primary data---the variables.
\pkg{gggda} implements a family of \emph{referential stats}, built on the prototype \texttt{StatRef}, that take a reference data frame as an additional argument.
This trick couples with a feature of \pkg{ordr} that preserves both matrix factors of an SVD-based technique so that each by default produces layers with the other as referent.

Finally, to evoke the common scale of the artificial axes, and to reveal the difference in data range between the first and second axes, biplot windows are often made square, rather than truncated in each dimension to the data range.
\pkg{gggda} provides a coordinatization ggproto \texttt{CoordRect} to achieve this for any desired ratio of window dimensions.
This in turn supports the in-development paneling ggproto \texttt{FacetOrd}, which yields a pairs plot for artificial coordinates that follows the rigid requirements above while permitting both fixed and varying panel sizes.
These conventions could be used to plot any set of real-valued data but to my knowledge have not previously been introduced.

\section{A Case for Domain-Constrained Grammar}

The case studies above lead me to argue that GoG, in theory as well as in practice, should take a deliberate approach to the constraint sets on statistical graphics associated with specific domains.
\pkg{ggplot2} itself deals delicately and implicitly with Simple Features data, for example, by transforming segments between geographic coordinates into approximate meridians and tucking the coordinatization constructor \texttt{coord\_sf()} into the geometric element constructor \texttt{geom\_sf()}; but users may violate good practice by overriding these behaviors.
At an opposite extreme, \pkg{ggraph} and \pkg{ggtern} are designed to operate entirely without native \pkg{ggplot2} constructors.
I suggest that a middle ground is required to satisfy the needs of users in, and especially those straddling, diverse specialties:
From each specialty should be derived a \emph{graphical dialect}, constituting both permitted objects and constraints on their combination.
Such dialects should be based on standards of good practice and formalized mathematically so that they may be unambiguously implemented.
At a low level, an implementation should then have the ability to declare and adopt a dialect, for a single plot (perhaps by using a special initializer) or for a section of code (perhaps by setting a global option), with the effect that plot assembly informatively enables domain-specific features and imposes domain-specific constraints (perhaps by code completions and suggestions).
At a high level, a family of well-defined dialects should help organize a grand unified GoG at an important meso-scale between its basic objects and its space of all feasible plot types.
